7. What Nexa Is Intended to Become

Nexa is envisioned as an integrated digital finance and commerce platform.

Its broader product direction includes several related components.

7.1 Digital Wallet

The wallet is intended to support account balances and financial transaction workflows.

Depending on the implemented version, these may include:

- Account balance information.
- Transaction history.
- Transfers between supported accounts.
- Deposit and withdrawal workflows.
- Notifications about relevant transaction events.
- Account and session management.

The wallet must be supported by backend logic that validates requests, enforces permissions, and preserves the integrity of financial records.

Displaying a balance is not sufficient evidence that the underlying financial operations are correct.

7.2 USDT and Blockchain Networks

The project's technical direction has included USDT-related functionality and networks such as TRON and BNB Smart Chain. Polygon has also been considered in the broader roadmap.

These networks have different technical requirements.

A working blockchain integration may need to address:

- Network-specific addresses.
- Deposit monitoring.
- Transaction confirmation.
- Withdrawal processing.
- Network fees.
- Transaction identifiers.
- Failed or delayed operations.
- Reconciliation between blockchain activity and internal records.

Support for one network does not imply support for every network.

Likewise, storing a transaction in a database does not prove that the corresponding blockchain transaction has been broadcast or confirmed.

Each supported network and transaction workflow must be evaluated against the actual implementation.

7.3 Internal Balances

Nexa may maintain internal balances for transactions conducted within the platform.

These balances need clear business rules.

The system must define how funds are credited or debited, how failed operations are handled, and how transaction records remain consistent with account balances.

Financial operations may also require database transactions, concurrency controls, constraints, and idempotency mechanisms.

The appropriate implementation depends on the specific transaction model and should be verified through source-code inspection and relevant tests.

7.4 Transaction History

Transaction history is intended to help users understand the activity associated with their accounts.

A useful history should accurately represent relevant transaction details and statuses.

Its information should be derived from the backend's actual state rather than assumptions made by the frontend.

Where notifications or real-time updates are implemented, they should also reflect authoritative server-side information.

8. Peer-to-Peer Trading: The Purpose of P2P

Peer-to-peer trading, commonly called P2P, is an important part of Nexa's broader vision.

P2P allows eligible participants to interact with other participants through defined transaction workflows.

Depending on the supported service, a transaction may involve a buyer and seller agreeing to exchange a digital asset or another supported form of value using an accepted payment method.

The exact assets, currencies, payment methods, and eligibility requirements depend on the implemented service and applicable legal requirements.

Why P2P?

P2P can provide several useful capabilities.

Offer discovery: Users can find and compare available offers.

Price comparison: Participants can evaluate prices and terms offered by different counterparties.

Structured orders: Transactions can follow a defined sequence rather than depend entirely on informal communication.

Transaction records: The platform can record the stages of an order and the relevant participant actions.

Dispute handling: Users can access a defined process for reporting problems.

Integration with commerce: P2P can complement other services when the business model and legal requirements permit it.

These capabilities can make transactions more organized, but they do not eliminate every risk.

A P2P interface cannot guarantee that a counterparty will fulfill an agreement or that a payment cannot be disputed.

The operating model must define the responsibilities of the participants and the platform.

A possible P2P workflow

A proposed workflow may include:

1. A seller publishes an offer with its terms and permitted payment methods.
2. A buyer selects the offer and creates an order.
3. The backend validates the order and checks relevant restrictions.
4. Where an authorized escrow mechanism exists, the relevant assets may be held or restricted according to the transaction rules.
5. The participants follow the agreed payment process.
6. The system evaluates the required confirmation or evidence.
7. The transaction is completed, cancelled, or referred to a dispute process according to the applicable rules.
8. The outcome is recorded.

This describes an intended workflow, not proof that every stage is already implemented.

The final design must reflect the actual custody model, supported payment methods, failure conditions, and legal requirements.

9. Why Nexa Is Intended to Act as an Intermediary

The platform is intended to provide digital infrastructure through which users can interact and conduct supported transactions.

For example, a marketplace can connect a buyer with an independent seller without the platform necessarily becoming the seller of every listed product.

A P2P service can provide offer discovery, order management, transaction records, and structured dispute handling.

The purpose is to organize interactions and clarify the responsibilities of buyers, sellers, and the platform.

However, being an intermediary does not automatically exempt a platform from financial or other legal obligations.

The actual requirements depend on the services provided, the degree of control over funds, the assets involved, the countries served, and the applicable regulatory framework.

Depending on the operating model, requirements may involve payment-service authorization, custody arrangements, identity verification, consumer protection, financial crime controls, data protection, or other obligations.

These matters must be assessed before launching regulated services.

The objective is to develop a platform whose actual operating model, responsibilities, and legal arrangements are clearly defined.

10. Escrow and Conditional Transactions

The broader Nexa vision includes an escrow-style mechanism for eligible transactions.

In a typical escrow workflow, funds or assets are held or restricted under predefined conditions while the parties complete their obligations.

For example, a buyer may place an order for a product. The relevant amount may remain unavailable to the seller until the required fulfillment condition is satisfied.

The intended purpose is to reduce certain risks and establish clearer expectations between participants.

A proper implementation must define:

- Who holds or controls the relevant assets.
- Which conditions permit release.
- How cancellations and refunds are handled.
- What happens when a participant stops responding.
- How disputes are initiated and reviewed.
- How duplicate requests are handled.
- How conflicting state changes are prevented.
- How transaction records are maintained and reconciled.

An escrow feature is not merely a database status.

If the assets are not actually held or restricted under the stated rules, the system must not imply that they are protected by escrow.

The final arrangement must also satisfy the legal requirements applicable to its custody and transaction model.

11. Marketplace and Seller Stores

Nexa is intended to provide a marketplace through which users can discover products and interact with sellers.

The broader design includes individual seller storefronts and product-management capabilities.

Depending on the implemented version, these may include:

- Store profiles and descriptions.
- Product names and descriptions.
- Product images.
- Prices and quantities.
- Inventory management.
- Delivery options.
- Order tracking.
- Seller information.
- Relevant transaction and fulfillment records.

The goal is to help sellers participate in digital commerce without requiring every seller to develop a separate online store and backend system.

A marketplace must also define how inventory is reserved, how orders progress, and how cancellations or refunds affect associated financial records.

Its reliability depends on the backend rules and operational procedures, not simply on the existence of product pages.

12. Orders, Fulfillment, and Delivery

The marketplace vision includes structured order management.

An order may pass through stages such as creation, payment processing, fulfillment, delivery confirmation, completion, cancellation, or dispute review.

The backend must define which transitions are permitted and which conditions must be satisfied.

Physical products and digital products require different fulfillment workflows.

For a physical product, fulfillment may involve delivery arrangements and confirmation of receipt.

For a digital product, fulfillment may involve authorizing access to a download or delivering an item electronically after the transaction has been validated.

These workflows should be designed around their actual failure conditions.

A user interface displaying an order as completed is not sufficient if the backend has not verified the relevant conditions.

13. Dispute Resolution

The Nexa vision includes a structured process for handling disagreements between participants.

A dispute may arise when a product is not delivered, a payment is disputed, or the participants disagree about whether an order has been fulfilled.

Depending on the final design, the process may include:

1. Opening a dispute.
2. Recording the reason and relevant evidence.
3. Restricting certain actions where appropriate.
4. Reviewing the available information.
5. Applying the outcome permitted by the platform's rules.
6. Recording the decision and resulting transaction state.

The existence of a dispute interface does not guarantee a fair outcome.

A reliable process requires defined rules, appropriate permissions, evidence handling, and a way to review relevant decisions.

Where funds or assets are involved, dispute handling must also respect the actual custody arrangement and applicable legal obligations.

14. Digital Products and Books

The broader marketplace vision includes digital products, potentially including downloadable books and other electronically delivered goods.

The intended workflow connects a validated purchase with access to the purchased item.

Such a system needs to enforce access rules on the server rather than trust a client-side claim that payment has occurred.

The product vision has also included exploring affordable digital books, with a proposed price ceiling of approximately US$0.50 for the relevant category.

This is a product-design proposal, not a claim that all digital books currently follow that pricing rule.

The actual catalog, pricing policy, and availability of digital products must be verified against the current implementation.

15. Currency Conversion and Pricing

Nexa's broader design includes exchange-rate functionality for displaying or calculating prices across supported currencies.

For example, a marketplace may need to convert a reference price into a locally displayed amount.

A sound implementation should define:

- The source of the exchange rate.
- Who may change it.
- When a new rate becomes effective.
- How rounding is handled.
- How fees or margins are disclosed.
- Whether a displayed conversion is indicative or binding.
- How existing orders are treated when rates change.
- How calculations can be reproduced for auditing.

If administrators can control an exchange-rate parameter, changes should be authorized and recorded.

Displaying a converted amount is not equivalent to executing a currency exchange.

Actual exchange services may require additional liquidity, settlement arrangements, operational controls, and regulatory authorization.

16. Deposits, Withdrawals, and Payment Providers

The broader financial-service vision includes potential integrations with payment providers and blockchain-related services.

An external provider may supply infrastructure for deposits, withdrawals, or other supported financial operations.

The precise responsibilities depend on the provider's API, contractual arrangement, permissions, and supported markets.

An integration is not complete simply because the application can send an HTTP request.

The implementation must handle authentication, request validation, failures, transaction status, duplicate callbacks, and reconciliation.

It must also consider cases where a request times out even though the external provider may have processed it.

Real-money services may require provider approval, commercial agreements, legal authorization, or other compliance measures.

The operational status of each integration must be established independently.

Such functionality would depend on the provider's availability, supported countries, identity requirements, commercial terms, and applicable regulations.

Nexa should not be described as independently issuing Visa cards unless the actual operating arrangement supports that statement.

If the functionality is supplied through a partner, the partnership and the responsibilities of each party should be described accurately.

18. Customer Support and AI-Assisted Features

The broader product vision includes customer-support functionality.

19. Accounts, Sessions, and Administration

The broader application design includes account management, session management, and administrative tools.

Depending on the implemented version, users may be able to review active sessions or devices and revoke access where supported by the authentication architecture.

Such controls must operate on actual server-side authentication state. Displaying a session list alone is not sufficient if the corresponding sessions cannot be reliably invalidated.

Administrative functionality may include reviewing users, transactions, products, orders, and disputes, as well as managing permitted platform settings.

Administrative actions require appropriate permissions and should be traceable.

A user interface is not proof that these controls are complete; their underlying authorization and behavior must be evaluated.

22. Ibrahim's Use of AI: Code Authorship and Scenario Analysis

Ibrahim states that he writes 100% of Nexa's application code himself.

He does not use AI to write, generate, or implement his project's code. He does not describe Nexa as a project assembled by copying AI-generated implementations or by copying code from open-source repositories.

His use of AI serves a different purpose: analyzing the behavior of code he has already written.

He provides his own project files and asks AI models to reason through hypothetical situations involving the system.

Examples include:

- What might happen if two requests attempt to modify the same account balance?
- What happens if a transaction fails between two database operations?
- How could a change in one component affect another component?
- What might occur if an external API becomes unavailable?
- Why could a particular sequence of operations produce an unexpected result?
- Which possible outcomes or failure scenarios should be considered?

The AI model responds with possible outcomes and explanations.

Ibrahim uses these explanations to broaden the range of scenarios he considers and to understand the mechanisms that may produce different results.

The motivation is practical. A complex application can contain many interacting conditions, and it is difficult for one person to enumerate every possible scenario mentally.

AI-assisted analysis can help explore those possibilities, but it is not infallible. A model can misunderstand code, overlook a condition, or produce an incorrect explanation.

Its output is therefore not treated as proof that the software is correct. Actual behavior must be established through the implementation and appropriate testing.

The distinction is fundamental:

Ibrahim writes the code himself and uses AI to analyze what that code might do under different conditions.

This section records his stated development process. It is not an independent audit of code authorship.

25. Project Status: Plans, Implementation, and Verification

Nexa is an ongoing project, and different components may have different levels of maturity.

For clarity, the following terms should be distinguished:

Planned: The feature belongs to the product vision but may not have been implemented.

In development: Work is taking place, but the feature may be incomplete.

Implemented: Relevant code exists, although its correctness and completeness still require evaluation.

Tested: Specific behavior has been tested under documented conditions.

Operational: The feature is deployed and functioning in its intended environment.

Production-ready: The feature has met the relevant engineering, operational, security, and compliance requirements for its intended use.

These categories are not interchangeable.

A feature should be described according to the evidence available at the time.

The same standard applies to payment-provider integrations, blockchain support, commercial partnerships, deployment claims, security statements, and user statistics.
